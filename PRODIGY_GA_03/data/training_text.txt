Artificial intelligence is the simulation of human intelligence processes by machines, especially computer systems. These processes include learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and language understanding. Machine learning is a foundational branch of artificial intelligence and computer science which focuses on the use of data and algorithms to imitate the way that humans learn, gradually improving its accuracy over time.

Generative artificial intelligence describes algorithms that can be used to create new content, including audio, synthetic code, images, text, simulations, and interactive media. Recent breakthroughs in the field have the potential to drastically change the approach to content creation, software engineering, and scientific research. Generative AI models are trained on patterns, statistical correlations, and structures within the input training data, enabling them to generate novel outputs that preserve the stylistic and semantic characteristics of the source corpus.

Deep learning is a specialized subset of machine learning based on artificial neural networks with representation learning. The adjective deep refers to the use of multiple layers in the network. These neural networks attempt to simulate the behavior of biological neurons in the human brain, allowing models to learn from massive amounts of unstructured data. While a neural network with a single hidden layer can make approximate predictions, additional hidden layers enable hierarchical feature extraction, capturing complex non-linear abstractions at each progressive layer.

Natural language processing is a subfield of computer science, artificial intelligence, and computational linguistics concerned with the interactions between computers and human language. In particular, it focuses on how to program computers to process, analyze, and synthesize large amounts of natural language data. The ultimate goal is a computer system capable of understanding the contents of documents, including the contextual nuances, sentiment, idioms, and syntactic structures of the language within them.

A Markov chain is a mathematical system that undergoes transitions from one state to another according to certain probabilistic rules. The defining characteristic of a Markov chain is the Markov property, also known as memorylessness. This principle states that the conditional probability distribution of future states depends solely upon the present state, and is independent of the sequence of events that preceded it. A countably infinite sequence, in which the chain moves state at discrete time intervals, defines a discrete-time Markov chain.

In the context of computational linguistics, character-level Markov chains treat each sequence of characters as a discrete state and predict the probability distribution of the next character based on observed transition frequencies. Early statistical language models heavily utilized n-gram Markov models to estimate likelihoods of words and character sequences. An n-gram is a contiguous sequence of n items from a given sample of text or speech. By counting the frequencies of character tuples, a Markov model can synthesize remarkably fluent text that mirrors the vocabulary, orthography, and grammatical rhythms of the training data.

Language models represent probability distributions over sequences of words or characters. By calculating the joint probability of a token sequence, language models enable tasks such as speech recognition, machine translation, spelling correction, and automated text completion. In character-level generation, the model learns the transition probabilities from a state of k characters to every possible successor character. For instance, given the character state "art", the model evaluates the empirical frequencies of characters that followed "art" in the training corpus, such as "i" in "artificial", "e" in "article", or "s" in "arts". It then selects the subsequent character through weighted random sampling.

Neural network architectures, particularly recurrent neural networks, long short-term memory networks, and transformer models, have transformed the landscape of generative artificial intelligence. Transformer models employ self-attention mechanisms to model relationships between tokens regardless of their positional distance. Despite the ascendancy of deep neural networks, classical probabilistic models like Markov chains provide essential theoretical foundations for understanding stochastic processes, state machines, transition matrices, and probabilistic text generation.

Supervised learning algorithms construct mathematical models from training datasets containing both input features and corresponding ground-truth labels. The algorithm iteratively optimizes its parameters to minimize a loss function, measuring the discrepancy between model predictions and true labels. Common supervised tasks include classification, regression, and sequence labeling.

Unsupervised learning algorithms discover hidden patterns, intrinsic geometry, and cluster structures in unlabelled datasets. Unlike supervised methods, unsupervised learning operates without human annotations. Common techniques include principal component analysis, k-means clustering, autoencoders, and density estimation. Generative modeling is closely aligned with unsupervised learning, as it seeks to capture the true underlying data distribution.

Reinforcement learning is an area of machine learning concerned with how intelligent software agents ought to take actions in an environment to maximize cumulative reward. The agent interacts with the environment through trial and error, balancing exploration of uncharted actions with exploitation of known rewarding strategies. Reinforcement learning has achieved historic milestones in game playing, autonomous robotics, automated trading, and alignment of generative language models through human feedback.

Computer vision is an interdisciplinary scientific field that deals with how computers can gain high-level understanding from digital images or videos. From the perspective of engineering, it seeks to automate tasks that the human visual system can perform. Computer vision tasks include acquiring, processing, analyzing, and understanding digital images to extract high-dimensional data from the real world in order to produce numerical or symbolic information.

Ethics in artificial intelligence is a critical domain addressing the moral implications, societal consequences, and governance of intelligent systems. Key ethical pillars include fairness, transparency, explainability, privacy protection, and accountability. As generative models become ubiquitous in society, addressing algorithmic bias, preventing the generation of misinformation, and ensuring data provenance are paramount challenges facing AI researchers and practitioners.

Statistical learning theory provides the mathematical framework for understanding machine learning algorithms from the fields of statistics and functional analysis. It addresses the problem of finding a predictive function based on data. The core objective is generalization, which measures how well a trained model performs on unseen data drawn from the same underlying probability distribution. Overfitting occurs when a model memorizes idiosyncrasies and noise in the training set rather than learning generalizable patterns.

Information theory, founded by Claude Shannon, quantifies the amount of information, entropy, and redundancy in communication systems. In language modeling, cross-entropy and perplexity serve as standard metrics to evaluate how well a probabilistic model predicts sample text. A lower perplexity indicates that the model is less surprised by the text, reflecting superior predictive performance and closer alignment with the empirical distribution.

Markov decision processes formalize decision making in situations where outcomes are partly random and partly under the control of a decision maker. They extend Markov chains by introducing actions and rewards, providing the mathematical backbone for modern reinforcement learning and optimal control algorithms.

In generative sequence modeling, the choice of Markov order represents a trade-off between randomness and memory. A first-order character model captures simple character pairings, producing abstract and phonetic pseudo-words. Higher-order models capture multi-character prefixes, morphemes, and whole words, yielding syntactically coherent phrases and intelligible grammatical structures. However, excessively high orders may lead to verbatim reproduction of training passages due to sparse state transitions. Finding the optimal model order balances creative synthesis with linguistic structure.
